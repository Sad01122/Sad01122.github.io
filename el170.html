The 10-Second Test — What Is a Clause?

Clause = a group of words that has BOTH a subject AND a verb.

WordsSubject?Verb?What is it?
Jim studiedJimstudied✅ clause
in the Sweet Shop✗ (no doer)✗✅ phrase, not a clause
because Jim studiedJimstudied✅ clause (but dependent!)
🧠
Trick: ask "WHO does the action?" (subject) and "WHAT is happening?" (verb). Both present → clause. Both words in your answer must be in the same group.

Independent vs Dependent Clause

Independent clause = it can live alone

A group of words with subject + verb that expresses a complete thought — it is a sentence.

Jim studied in the Sweet Shop for his chemistry quiz. ✅ complete — standalone.

💡
"So what?" test: read the group of words alone. If your mind asks "…and then? What happened?" it is NOT complete → not independent.

Dependent clause = it needs a partner

Has subject + verb but does NOT express a complete thought — it cannot be a sentence. Usually starts with a dependent marker word.

When Jim studied in the Sweet Shop for his chemistry quiz… 😕 So what? → dependent. Alone it dangles → that's a fragment error.

Dependent Marker Words — Grouped by Meaning

GroupMarkersExample
⏰ TIMEafter, before, when, whenever, while, since, until, onceAfter I submitted the assignment, I slept.
❓ REASONbecause, as, sinceBecause it was raining, we stayed inside.
⚖️ CONDITIONif, unless, whether, even ifIf you revise daily, the quiz will feel easy.
🔄 CONTRASTalthough, though, even though, whileAlthough he studied all night, he failed.
📍 PLACE/OTHERwhere, wherever, that, which, whoThe book that I borrowed is late.
🧠
Shortcut: if the sentence starts with one of these markers, it's almost certainly a dependent clause → you MUST attach it to a complete sentence. Grouping by meaning beats memorizing one giant list.

Joining Two Independent Clauses — Only 5 Legal Ways

#MethodExample
1Period (two sentences)I like this class. It is very interesting.
2Semicolon ; (close ideas)I like this class; it is very interesting.
3Comma + FANBOYSI like this class, and it is very interesting.
4Make one clause dependentI like this class because it is very interesting.
5Combine the ideasThis interesting class is one I like.
🔑
FANBOYS = the 7 coordinating conjunctions: For · And · Nor · But · Or · Yet · So. Comma goes before the FANBOYS word when it joins two complete sentences: Jim studied for his quiz, but it was hard to concentrate.

Independent marker words (start a sentence, join with ;): also, however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, consequently, finally, instead, then. Structure: ; + however, + sentence.

The Three Classic Errors (+ Every Fix)

⚠️
❌ Comma splice — a comma alone joining two complete sentences.
Wrong: I like this class, it is very interesting.
✅ Five fixes: 1) I like this class. It is very interesting. · 2) I like this class; it is very interesting. · 3) I like this class, and it is very interesting. · 4) I like this class because it is very interesting. · 5) Because it is very interesting, I like this class.
⚠️
❌ Fused sentence / run-on — two sentences, NO punctuation.
Wrong: My professor is intelligent I've learned a lot from her.
✅ …intelligent. I've learned… · …intelligent; I've learned… · …intelligent, and I've learned… · …intelligent; moreover, I've learned…
⚠️
❌ Fragment — a dependent clause pretending to be a sentence.
Wrong: Because I forgot the exam was today.
✅ Because I forgot the exam was today, I didn't study. · I forgot the exam was today.
✅
Detection trick: two or more subject–verb pairs in one sentence with no punctuation → run-on. Sentence starts with because/while/which/who/until… → check it's attached → else fragment.

Comma Golden Rule (memorize this one line)

💡
Dependent clause FIRST → comma. Independent clause FIRST → NO comma.
✅ After I finished, I watched a movie. · ✅ I watched a movie after I finished.
Same for FANBOYS: complete-sentence, and complete-sentence.

The One Test — Phrase vs Clause

Has subject + verb?Can stand alone?Example
Phrase❌ never both❌ neverin the morning · the tall man in a blue jacket
Clause✅ alwaysindependent ✅ / dependent ❌She eats eggs · because she eats eggs
🧠
Nesting trick: 📦 phrases live inside clauses, clauses live inside sentences.
She eats eggs in the morning. → clause She eats eggs + phrase in the morning.
Fast test: point at a group of words — "WHO does the action?" If no subject doing a verb → phrase. Meows so loudly 😕 (who?) → phrase. That cat meows so loudly ✅ → clause.

The HEAD Trick — Every Phrase Has One Boss Word

the tall man in a blue jacket ↓ ↓ ↑ ↓ det adj HEAD postmodifier
💡
Cover everything except the head — what's left is the phrase's type & job.
the tall man in a blue jacket → cover modifiers → man (a noun) → noun phrase.
aware of the danger → aware (an adjective) → adjective phrase.
very well → well (an adverb) → adverb phrase.

The Four Phrase Types

TypeFormulaExampleIts job?
🧢 Noun Phrase (NP)(Det) + (Adj) + HEAD NOUN + (Post)the tall man in a blue jacket · a feeling of isolationSubject / object / object of preposition
📐 Prepositional (PP)Preposition + noun phraseunder the table (WHERE) · after noon (WHEN) · on time (HOW)Usually adverb job (answers where/when/how)
🎨 Adjective (AdjP)(Intensifier) + ADJ + (Complement) — no subject–verbvery cold · aware of the danger · a big square pink boxWhich one? / what kind?

🔑
Job tests: replace the phrase with he/she/it → still works = noun's job. Ask "which one? / what kind?" → answer = adjective's job (Cats from the alleys… → WHICH cats?). Ask when/where/how/why → answer = adverb's job (She eats bread in the morning = WHEN).

The Trap: Adjective Phrase vs Adjective Clause

Adj phraseAdj clause
subject + verb?❌✅
exampleafraid of dogswho didn't pass inspection
in a sentenceThe dog afraid of thunder hid.My car, which didn't pass inspection, broke down.
✅
Rule: a clause has a mini-sentence inside it (subject+verb); a phrase doesn't. That's the whole test.

Common Mistakes (❌ wrong / ✅ right)

  • ❌ Standalone phrase = fragment — In the morning. (as a short answer to "when?" it's fine; as a sentence, no.) ✅ I run in the morning.
  • ❌ Double head — The box is on the table is broken. ✅ The box on the table is broken.
  • ❌ Fake subject in a phrase — he in the morning. ✅ clause needs a real verb: he runs in the morning.
  • ✅ Ending with a preposition (Put it on.) is fine in modern English — but The table is on is incomplete → ✅ The table is on the ground.

🧠 The Big Trick — It's Not 12 Rules, It's a 3 × 4 Grid

SIMPLE
(a fact)		CONTINUOUS
(in progress)		PERFECT
(already done)		PERF. CONTINUOUS
(how long)
PRESENTI workI am workingI have workedI have been working
PASTI workedI was workingI had workedI had been working
FUTUREI will workI will be workingI will have workedI will have been working
🧠
Three tricks inside the grid:
1) The name builds the formula: past perfect continuous = past (had) + perfect (been) + continuous (-ing) → had been working.
2) Aspects never change meaning: simple = fact/habit · continuous = be+ing (in progress) · perfect = have+V3 (done; links times) · PC = have been+ing (duration).
3) Time only changes the helping verb: present = (nothing)/have · past = had/was-were · future = will.
💡
Picture trick — draw a timeline: PAST —— 📍NOW ——→ FUTURE. Continuous = a wavy line (action stretched out) · Perfect = an arrow pointing back (finished before another time) · Perfect continuous = wavy line + arrow (duration up to a point).

🟢 Present Tenses — Use · Formula · Signal · Trap

TenseWhen to useFormulaSignal wordsExample
Present Simplehabits, facts, universal truthsV1 (+s/es he/she/it); do/does for Q/Nalways, usually, every day, neverShe reads books daily. The sun rises in the east.
Present Continuoushappening now, temporary actions, arrangementsam/is/are + V-ingnow, at the moment, right nowShe is reading a novel now.
Present Perfectcompleted actions with present relevance, experienceshas/have + V3just, already, yet, ever, never, since, forI have finished my homework.
Present Perfect Cont.started in past, continues now (duration focus)has/have been + V-ingfor, since, all day, how longShe has been teaching for five years.
⚠️
Traps: ❌ I am teaching in university (permanent) → ✅ I teach at the university · ❌ He work → ✅ He works · ❌ I have seen him yesterday → ✅ I saw him yesterday · ❌ I am working here since 2015 → ✅ I have been working here since 2015 · ❌ I am living in Lahore (permanent) → ✅ I live in Lahore.

🔑
"Can you name when?" test: specific finished time → past simple (I lived there in 2019); unspecified / still true now → present perfect (I have lived here for 5 years). for = length of time · since = starting point (since 2023).

🧩 Building Tricks — Questions, Negatives, -s

  • First helping verb moves in questions/negatives: Has she been studying? · She hasn't been studying.
  • Only simple present & past need do/does/did — every other tense already has an auxiliary: Does she study? · Is she studying? · Will she study?
  • Third-person -s: he/she/it → works, goes, teaches (#1 spoken-English mistake worldwide).
  • After did, use V1: Did you go ✅ — Did you went ❌.
  • Stative verbs (know, understand, love, want, need, seem) → never continuous: I understand ✅ — I am understanding ❌.

📚 Learn These First + Practice That Works

✅
Research-backed order: 1) Present Simple → 2) Present Continuous → 3) Past Simple → 4) will / going to → 5) Present Perfect — these five cover ~95% of everyday English. Then: Past Continuous → Past Perfect → Present Perfect Continuous → future continuous/perfect last (rare, even for natives).
  • One-verb drill: take study and fill every grid cell: I study · I am studying · I studied · I have studied… then repeat with verbs from YOUR life (work, play, cook).
  • Rewrite a paragraph in the past, then in the future (forces tense switching).
  • Cover the examples in the tables above and make your own sentence per tense — passive reading → active grammar.
