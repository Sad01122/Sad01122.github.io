🧱 Clauses — Independent vs Dependent

IndependentDependent
Hassubject + verb + complete thoughtsubject + verb + a marker word
Can stand alone?Yes — it's a sentenceNo — needs an independent partner
Examplethe sun was shiningbecause the sun was shining

Marker words by meaning: TIME (after, before, when, while, since, until) · REASON (because, as, since) · CONDITION (if, unless, whether) · CONTRAST (although, though, even though).

🧠
FANBOYS = For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So → join two independent clauses with comma + FANBOYS.
Transition words (however, therefore, moreover) join with ; + word,.
5 fixes for a comma splice / run-on / fragment: period · semicolon · comma+FANBOYS · make one clause dependent · combine into one sentence.

6-part anatomy: ① subject line · ② greeting · ③ purpose in the first 1–2 lines · ④ body (one idea per paragraph) · ⑤ closing ask with a deadline · ⑥ sign-off + signature.

💡
5-step frame with BLUF = Bottom Line Up Front: Subject (Leave request: 19–21 October) → Greeting → Purpose first ("I am writing to request…") → Details + ask + deadline ("Please reply by Friday") → Close ("Kind regards").

You-attitude: ✗ "We cannot refund without a receipt." → ✓ "Return the item with your receipt to receive a full refund."

Openers: "Thank you for your email of 14 June." · "I am writing to enquire about…" · "Further to our phone call…" · "As you requested, I am attaching…"
Closers: "I look forward to hearing from you." · "Please feel free to contact me."

⚠️
Never in formal mail: Hi/Hello opener · Cheers/Thanks!/TTYL closing · slang, emojis, ALL CAPS · text-speak (u, asap, thx) · comma splices · forget the attachment. Do: specific subject · proofread · check the recipient field · reply promptly.

6 email kinds: internal · external · sales · routine · personalized · circulars. Letter types: personal · inquiry (intro/body/conclusion) · complaint · sales (headline→intro→body→call to action).

🎭 FE Mids Mock Test — 15 MCQs

⏱️ ~15 min🎯 aim ≥ 12/154 tenses · 3 clauses · 2 phrases · 2 PoS · 2 email · 2 cross-culture ✅ instant marking + explanations

1She ___ to school every day. (go)Tenses

Answer: B — goes. "Every day" = habit → present simple, and he/she/it takes -s. ❌ go forgets the -s · going needs is · gone is V3.

2By the time we reached the station, the train ___. (leave)Tenses

Answer: C — had left. Two past actions: the train left before we reached → the earlier one takes the past perfect (had + V3); the later one stays past simple.

3I ___ my keys, so I cannot open the door now. (lose)Tenses

Answer: C — have lost. Past action with a result right now (I still can't get in) → present perfect. Pure past story time would be lost.

4Look! The kids ___ in the garden. (play)Tenses

Answer: B — are playing. Signal word "Look!" = happening at this moment → present continuous (am/is/are + -ing).

5"Because it rained heavily, the match was cancelled." — the underlined part is a…Clauses

Answer: B — dependent clause. It has a subject (it) + verb (rained) → so it's a clause; the marker because makes it dependent (cannot stand alone).

6I was very tired, ___ I kept working.Clauses

Answer: B — but. The two halves contrast (tired vs kept working). A comma + FANBOYS joins two independent clauses; because/although would need a full dependent clause after them.

7Which sentence is a comma splice?Clauses

Answer: B. Two independent clauses joined by only a comma = comma splice. ✅ (a) comma+FANBOYS · (c) semicolon · (d) one clause made dependent.

8"The cat hid under the table." That phrase is a…Phrases

Answer: D — prepositional phrase. Head word = under (a preposition), followed by its object. It tells where (an adverb-like job), but the head decides the name.

9"The very tall boy." — the phrase is a…Phrases

Answer: B. Inside the phrase, the head is tall (adjective) and very only intensifies it. Boy is outside the phrase — it's the noun the phrase describes.

10In "She spoke honestly" — honestly is an…Parts of Speech

Answer: A — adverb. It describes the verb "spoke" (how?) and ends in -ly. Adjectives describe nouns/pronouns, not verbs.

11Which of these words is a preposition?Parts of Speech

Answer: A — beneath. It shows place/relationship before a noun ("beneath the tree"). Rapid = adjective · rapidly = adverb (-ly) · rapidity = noun (-ity).

12You do not know the recipient's name. The correct formal greeting is…Email

Answer: B. Formal mail never opens with Hi/Hello/Hey. If you know the name use Dear Mr Smith,; if not, Dear Sir or Madam, (or "To Whom It May Concern").

13In the 5-step formal email frame, BLUF stands for…Email

Answer: B — Bottom Line Up Front. Put your purpose and the action first, details after (Sehgal, HBR 2016). Busy readers only read the first lines.

14The Cultural Iceberg model of culture was given by…Cross-Culture

Answer: B — Hall (1976). The classic mix-up is crediting Hofstede — his hidden-core picture is the onion (Symbols → Heroes → Rituals → Values).

15Pakistan scores 14 and the USA 91 on which Hofstede dimension?Cross-Culture

Answer: C — Individualism vs Collectivism. 14 = strongly collectivist (group before self) vs USA 91 = individualist. The other pairs are the distractors' own scores: 70/46 = uncertainty avoidance, 55/40 = power distance.

Q7 · Scenario Question — Identify the ICEBERG Model Terms (10 marks)

scenario
A Dutch manager joins a Pakistani company. At his first meeting he asks, "Anyone see a problem with this plan?" — no junior staff member speaks; the senior manager speaks last and everyone simply agrees with him. 🔁 Converse · Inverse · Contrapositive (your hint topic!)

Start from any "if…then": p → q ("If it rains, the ground is wet").

NameShapeEnglishEquivalent to p→q?

🔑
The golden rule (almost always asked!): the contrapositive is logically equivalent to the original. The converse and inverse are equivalent to each other — but NOT to the original. Quick memory: "Contra- = correct flip · Con- = the wrong way · In- = same wrong way as Con-."
💡
Why: p→q ≡ ¬q→¬p — flip and negate both sides once = safe. Just flipping (converse) or just negating (inverse) gives a different statement. Trap example: "If an animal is a cat, it has 3 legs" (false!) — its converse "if it has 3 legs it's a cat" is also false, but the contrapositive "if it does NOT have 3 legs it is NOT a cat" is safely true with the original.

Q1 · Function parts (2)

Define a function. Explain why defining a function does not execute it, and name the three forms involved in using one.

Model answer

A function is a named, reusable block of statements that performs one task. It runs only when it is called; a definition merely tells the compiler what the body is. The three forms are the prototype (announcement), the call (execution), and the definition (the actual body).

Q2 · Argument, parameter & pass-by-value (2)

Differentiate an argument from a parameter. What exactly does pass-by-value copy, and what happens to the caller's variable if the parameter is changed?

Model answer

An argument is the value supplied in the function call; a parameter is the receiving variable in the function header. Arguments are matched to parameters by position. Pass-by-value copies the argument's value into the parameter, so changing the parameter changes only the copy — the caller's original variable is unchanged.

Q3 · Scope vs lifetime (2)

State the difference between scope and lifetime. A static int count = 0; is declared inside visit(). What are its scope and lifetime, and what does three calls print?

Model answer

Scope = where the name is visible/usable. Lifetime = how long its storage exists. A static local has local scope (visible only inside visit()) but program lifetime; it is initialized once and keeps its value, so three calls print 1, 2, 3.

Q4 · Overloading rules (2)

Why is int f(); double f(); not valid overloading? Give one valid overload pair and say how the compiler chooses between them.

Model answer

Overloading requires different parameter lists. The call does not state a return type, so the compiler cannot choose between two functions that differ only by return type — the second declaration is a redeclaration error. Valid: int add(int a, int b); and double add(double a, double b);. The compiler selects the overload from the number and types of the arguments.

Q5 · inline, return and exit() (2)

Distinguish inline, return and exit(). Which one terminates the entire program, and which one is only a request to the compiler?

Model answer

inline is a request to expand a small function at the call site — the compiler may ignore it. return ends the current function and returns control to its caller. exit() (from <cstdlib>) terminates the entire program immediately. So: program-wide = exit(); request only = inline.

Q6 · GCD and LCM — recursive + overflow-safe (7) Hard

Write a recursive int gcd(int a, int b) (Euclid) that handles negatives, then long long lcm(int a, int b) that returns 0 if either input is 0 and avoids overflow by dividing before multiplying. Trace gcd(48, 18).

Model answer

#include <iostream> using namespace std; int gcd(int a, int b) { if (a < 0) a = -a; if (b < 0) b = -b; if (b == 0) return a; // base case return gcd(b, a % b); // smaller problem } long long lcm(int a, int b) { if (a == 0 || b == 0) return 0; int g = gcd(a, b); long long result = (long long)(a / g) * b; // divide first return result < 0 ? -result : result; } int main() { cout << gcd(48, 18) << "\n"; // 6 cout << lcm(48, 18) << "\n"; // 144 return 0; }

Trace: gcd(48,18) → gcd(18,12) → gcd(12,6) → gcd(6,0) = 6. Unwinding returns the same 6 up the stack.

Overflow: a * b can overflow even when the LCM fits. a / gcd(a,b) is exact, so divide first and only then multiply. LCM check: 48/6 × 18 = 144.

Q8 · Pascal's triangle — no arrays (8) Hard

Scenario. A university portal lets students log in with email + password. There is no MFA and no rate limit. A leaked password list from an unrelated gaming site is replayed against the portal; 412 accounts open. Logs show one IP address trying 60,000 combinations overnight. One attacker changes a student’s bank details and downloads fee records.

(a) Identify the asset, threat, vulnerability and risk. (b) Which CIA properties failed? (c) Give two controls that would have stopped the takeover and one that would detect it.

Model answer

(a) Asset = student accounts and fee/identity records. Threat = external cybercriminal using a credential-stuffing tool. Vulnerability = password reuse accepted by the portal, no MFA, no rate limiting. Risk = account takeover → fee fraud + exposure of student data (high likelihood, high impact).

(b) Confidentiality (records downloaded) and integrity (bank details changed). Availability is not the main loss here.

(c) Preventive: MFA (preferably phishing-resistant), breached-password blocking, rate limiting + bot detection. Detective: alert on many failed logins / one IP hitting many accounts / impossible travel, and notify the user on new-device login.

Examiner wants: the link password reuse → no MFA → takeover, not a generic “use a strong password”.

Q2 · “Hi, I’m the new manager” (5)

Scenario. A hotel chain’s help desk receives a call from a man claiming to be a new regional manager. He knows the manager’s name, office and start date from LinkedIn, answers the “security questions” from social media, and asks for an MFA reset because “my phone is broken”. The reset succeeds. Within an hour, the account adds a new admin user; ransomware is deployed across reservation servers two days later.

(a) Name two social-engineering techniques in use. (b) Map the incident to the attack chain and give one control at three different stages. (c) Why is “annual security-awareness training” not a sufficient fix?

Model answer

(a) Pretexting (invented new-manager story), vishing (voice call), plus OSINT from LinkedIn/social media and impersonation.

(b) Recon (LinkedIn/social media) → initial access (help-desk call) → execution (admin account + ransomware) → impact. Controls: minimise public role data and monitor exposure; enforce out-of-band identity proofing + callback to a known number before any MFA reset; least privilege for help-desk staff, alert on new admin creation, EDR and segmentation.

(c) The attack targets a process, not employee ignorance. A well-trained agent can still be fooled when the policy allows a reset after guessable questions. Fix the identity-proofing procedure and technical authority limits, then train.

Examiner wants: process failure + out-of-band verification, not “tell users not to click links”.

Q3 · The urgent invoice (5)

Scenario. Finance receives an email from billing@supp1ier.com (note the “1”) with a PDF invoice: “Our bank changed — pay the new account before month-end or the shipment is delayed.” The real supplier’s domain is supplier.com. The email passes SPF and DKIM because the look-alike domain has its own valid records. An employee updates the bank details and pays PKR 4.2 million.

(a) Name the attack and three red flags. (b) Why do SPF/DKIM/DMARC not prevent this? (c) Design a process control that would stop the payment.

Model answer

(a) Business email compromise / invoice fraud (a targeted phishing variant). Red flags: look-alike domain, urgency + threat of loss, change of bank details, unexpected sender, PDF-only instruction.

(b) SPF/DKIM/DMARC authenticate the domain that actually sent the mail. The attacker owns supp1ier.com and can publish valid records for it. They do not prove the sender is the real supplier. DMARC alignment would only reject if the attacker spoofed supplier.com itself.

(c) Dual approval for bank-detail changes + callback verification to a number already on file + freeze/settlement delay for new accounts. Never confirm using the phone number or reply-to in the email.

Examiner wants: distinction between domain authentication and supplier identity verification.

Section B · Malware, OS & Network Scenarios (20 marks)

Q4 · The hospital that went back to paper (5)

Scenario. A nurse opens a “lab results” attachment. A macro runs PowerShell, which downloads a payload. The malware steals saved credentials, moves over SMB to file servers, and encrypts shared folders. The hospital’s backups are on a mapped drive that is also encrypted. Staff switch to paper; appointments are cancelled for three days; a ransom note demands cryptocurrency.

(a) Classify the malware and name two propagation techniques. (b) Which CIA properties are hit, and which one justifies paying or not paying? (c) Name one preventive, one detective and one recovery control, and explain why the existing backup failed.

Model answer

(a) Ransomware delivered by a trojan/macro loader; propagation via phishing attachment and lateral SMB movement with stolen credentials.

(b) Availability is definitely lost (systems/files unavailable). Confidentiality is at risk because credentials were stolen and data may have been exfiltrated before encryption; integrity of records is also at risk. Availability is the ransom lever, but patient-safety impact is the priority.

(c) Preventive: email attachment/macro blocking, patching, least privilege, EDR, segmentation. Detective: central logging/EDR alert on PowerShell + mass file rename/encryption. Recovery: offline, immutable, tested backups + incident-response and downtime plan. The mapped-drive backup failed because it was online and reachable with the same credentials, so the ransomware encrypted it too.

Examiner wants: “backup exists” is not enough — it must be isolated and restorable.

Q5 · The lab image with a secret (5)

Scenario. A university lab uses one local-admin password on every PC, documented in a shared text file. A student finds it, installs a keylogger, and captures a lecturer’s domain credentials. The student changes grades and deletes the audit log on the lab PC. No central logging exists.

(a) Which security principles failed? (b) List four concrete OS-hardening changes. (c) How would logging/accounting have changed the outcome?

Model answer

(a) Least privilege (students had admin), separation of duties (one shared secret), secure defaults, and weak accounting (local logs deletable).

(b) Unique local-admin password per machine or LAPS; students use standard accounts; remove unnecessary services/software; patch OS; application allow-listing; full-disk encryption; central logging with tamper protection; block keylogger-style drivers where possible.

(c) Central logs would record the admin logon, process creation, grade-change action and log deletion attempt, with user/host/time. Alerts on privilege use and grade changes would identify the student, and off-box logs would survive local deletion.

Examiner wants: link shared admin → credential theft → no attribution; controls at identity, endpoint and logging layers.

Q6 · Free airport Wi-Fi (5)

Scenario. A traveller joins Free_Airport_WiFi. A captive portal asks for email and a “certificate install” to continue. The attacker runs an access point with the same name, intercepts DNS, and serves a fake staff-login page. The traveller’s session cookie is stolen and used to open the company webmail; the internal app still uses HTTP.

(a) Name the attack types and the network layer/stage. (b) Does HTTPS completely protect the traveller? Explain. (c) Give one control for the traveller and two for the airport/company.

Model answer

(a) Evil twin / rogue access point, on-path (MITM) attack, DNS spoofing and credential/session theft. It starts at the network access layer and moves to the application layer.

(b) Not completely. HTTPS protects traffic to the real server, but a fake portal can present its own certificate and phish the password; users click through warnings, and a stolen session cookie may be replayed if it is not bound to the device. HTTP internal apps expose everything on the path.

(c) Traveller: use mobile data/VPN, verify the SSID, disable auto-join, never install unknown certificates. Airport: WPA3/802.1X, rogue-AP monitoring, network segmentation, DNS filtering. Company: MFA + conditional access, HTTPS/HSTS everywhere, short-lived device-bound sessions.

Examiner wants: “HTTPS is not a substitute for trusting the network”.

Q7 · The invoice URL that counted to a million (5)

Scenario. A telecom exposes GET /api/customer/104. Any logged-in user can change the number and receive another customer’s name, address, CNIC and plan. There is no rate limit. An attacker scripts IDs 1→1,000,000 and downloads 10 million records. The API returns 200 for every request, and monitoring only watches server uptime.

(a) Name the vulnerability class and the failed security property. (b) Why is “the user was authenticated” not a defence? Which authorisation model would help? (c) Give three controls and one metric that would detect the attack.

Model answer

(a) IDOR / broken object-level authorisation (BOLA). Confidentiality failed; integrity is at risk if records can be modified. Authentication succeeded, authorisation did not.

(b) Authentication only proves who the caller is; it does not prove the caller may access this object. Use server-side object-level checks, with RBAC/ABAC or ownership rules (customer can read only their own record).

(c) Random/UUID identifiers (not a fix alone), per-object authorisation, rate limiting and anomaly detection, WAF/API gateway, encrypted data at rest. Metric: number of distinct object IDs requested per user per minute, or a spike in 403/200 responses for sequential IDs.

Examiner wants: the phrase “authenticated ≠ authorised” and object-level checks.

Section C · Capstone (5 marks)

Q8 · Security plan for a 20-person fintech (5)

Scenario. “PayLink” is a 20-person startup with a cloud web app, remote staff, customer CNIC and bank data, one IT admin, no written policies, no MFA, unpatched internet-facing servers, and nightly backups to the same cloud account. Investors ask for a 90-day security plan before funding.

(a) Rank the top three risks and justify each with likelihood × impact. (b) Map four controls across people, process and technology. (c) Give a 30/60/90-day sequence and one metric to prove progress.

Model answer

(a) ① Credential theft → cloud takeover (high likelihood: no MFA; high impact: all customer data) — highest. ② Unpatched internet-facing server exploited (medium-high likelihood, high impact). ③ Over-privileged single admin / insider (medium likelihood, high impact, weak detection).

(b) People: MFA + phishing training for all staff, security owner. Process: access-review and patch-management policy, incident-response runbook, vendor checklist. Technology: MFA/SSO, patching, least-privilege IAM, central logging, immutable off-account backups, encryption.

(c) Days 0–30: MFA everywhere, inventory assets, patch critical internet-facing systems, isolate backups. 31–60: least-privilege IAM, logging/alerts, IR plan, staff training. 61–90: tabletop exercise, access review, penetration test, report to investors. Metric: % accounts with MFA, % critical patches within 7 days, mean time to detect a simulated alert.

Examiner wants: prioritisation by risk, not a wish-list; coverage of people + process + technology; a measurable sequence.
