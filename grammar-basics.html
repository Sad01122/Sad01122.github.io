What Is a Part of Speech?

Every word in a sentence has one job — its part of speech. There are 8 of them, and tomorrow's quiz will ask you to name the job of a word in a sentence.

🧠
Memory name: IVAN CAPP — Interjections · Verbs · Adverbs · Nouns · Conjunctions · Adjectives · Pronouns · Prepositions.
Say it out loud twice — it sticks for the exam hall.
🔑
The 150-year-old classroom rhyme (Tower & Tweed) — abridged:
Every name is called a NOUN… In place of noun the PRONOUN stands… The ADJECTIVE describes a thing… The VERB means action, something done… How things are done the ADVERBS tell… The PREPOSITION shows relation… CONJUNCTIONS join, in many ways… The INTERJECTION cries out, "Hark!"

One sentence, eight possible jobs — find each word's role
💡
Quiz tip: you're never asked about the word in isolation — always its job in THAT sentence. Light is a noun in "Light travels fast" but an adjective in "a light bag". Position decides everything.

⚡ The 4 Swap-Tests (identify any word in 5 seconds)

1️⃣ Swap for a name → still fits? pronoun 2️⃣ Add "very" → fits? adjective (noun still there) 3️⃣ Add "how / when / where"? → you need an adverb 4️⃣ Can you insert "do" or the action happens? verb
  • -ly test: adjective + -ly = adverb (quick → quickly). Exceptions: friendly, lovely, early are adjectives!
  • Remove test: delete the word — sentence still grammatical but less detailed? Often an adjective ("a red car" → "a car").
  • and-test: can you put and between it and a noun or another clause? → adjective pair or conjunction.
  • Position test: before a noun showing place/time → preposition ("in the box"); before a verb → could be adverb or helping verb.

⚠️
#1 exam trap: the SAME word can be two parts of speech — always classify by position + job in the sentence, never by the word alone. "I need a book" (noun) vs "Book the ticket" (verb).

⚡ Quick Deep-Dives — Verbs · Adverbs · Conjunctions · Interjections

Verb — the engine of the sentence

  • Action verbs: run, write, eat · Linking verbs: am/is/are/was/were, seem, become, look (link subject to description → take an adjective: "She looks happy").
  • Helping (auxiliary) verbs: be / have / do + modals (can, will, must) — they help the main verb build tenses: is eating · has finished · did go.
  • Only simple present & past need do/does/did for questions/negatives: Does she study? · Did you go? (never "Did you went?").
  • Third-person -s: he/she/it → works, goes, teaches — the #1 spoken mistake worldwide.

Adverb — describes how / when / where / how much

  • Most end in -ly (quickly, sadly) but not all: very, often, always, here, soon, fast.
  • Frequency adverbs sit before the main verb, after "be": She always arrives early. · He is always late.
  • Adjective vs adverb: "She sang sweetly" (how → adverb) vs "a sweet song" (which kind of noun → adjective).

Conjunction — the connector

TypeMembersJob
CoordinatingFANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, Sojoin two equal clauses: I studied, and I passed.
Subordinatingbecause, although, if, when, while, since, until, unlessattach a dependent clause: Although he studied, he failed.

Interjection — the emotional blip

Stands alone, shows surprise/pain/joy, usually with !: Oh! · Wow! · Alas! · Ouch! · Hmm. Doesn't join the grammar of the sentence — it's punctuation-level emotion.

🧬 Open vs Closed Classes (fun fact that shows up in MCQs)

✅
Open classes (language keeps adding words): nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs — think: selfie, google, livestream.
Closed classes (almost never grow): pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions, interjections + articles. English has used in, on, and, he for centuries — they're the grammar glue.

What Is an Adjective?

An adjective describes a noun or pronoun — it answers which one? · what kind? · how many?

the red balloon → which? a tall building → what kind? five students → how many?
💡
Mini-test: put the word very in front. very red · very tall · very five ✗ — if "very ___" sounds okay, it's almost certainly an adjective.

Where Do Adjectives Go? (2 positions)

PositionPatternExample
Before the noun (attributive)(article) + ADJ + nouna happy child · the red car
After a linking verb (predicative)subject + linking verb + ADJThe child is happy. · She seems tired.
🔑
Linking verbs (adjective follows them): be (is/am/are/was/were) · seem · look · feel · become · sound · taste · appear. ❌ She looks happily (adverb!) → ✅ She looks happy.

👑 Royal Order of Adjectives (the OSASCOMP trick)

When several adjectives stack before a noun, English speakers always order them like this — wrong order sounds "off" even with perfect spelling:

1 Opinion→ 2 Size→ 3 Age→ 4 Shape→ 5 Colour→ 6 Origin→ 7 Material→ 8 Purpose→ ✏️ NOUN
🧠
Mnemonic: OSASCOMP — Opinion Size Age Shape Colour Origin Material Complement/Purpose.
Worked example: a lovely (opinion) little (size) old (age) round (shape) blue (colour) Pakistani (origin) wooden (material) dining (purpose) table ✅
❌ a wooden blue old lovely Pakistani dining round little table — every piece is true, but the order is chaos.
✅
Super-short rule (Cambridge): opinions come before facts. Your opinion (lovely) first, undisputable facts (blue, wooden) later. Determiners (the, my, these, five) still come before everything.

⚠️ The -ed vs -ing Trap (#1 adjective mistake)

🧠
People FEEL -ed. Things ARE -ing. The cause is -ing; the feeler is -ed.
The lecture is boring. → I am bored. ✅
The movie is exciting. → We are excited. ✅
❌ I am boring (= people find me dull 😅) · ❌ The movie is bored (= the movie has feelings).

What Is a Pronoun?

A pronoun replaces a noun so you don't repeat it:

Maya forgot Maya's bag 😕 → Maya forgot her bag ✅
💡
Golden habit: before choosing a pronoun, ask its job — is it doing the action (subject), receiving it (object), showing possession, or pointing back at the subject? Job decides the form.

⚔️ The Classic Pronoun Battles

⚠️
Battle 1 — "Me and him went" trap. ❌ Me and him went to the park. ✅ He and I went to the park.
Solo test: remove the other person — "Me went" 😬 obviously wrong → it must be "He and I went". Politeness order: put yourself last (He and I, not I and he).
⚠️
Battle 2 — its vs it's. Its (no apostrophe) = possession: The dog wagged its tail. It's = it is / it has only: It's raining. ❌ The dog hurt it's paw.
Trick: apostrophe = something was left out. Possession leaves nothing out → no apostrophe (same for hers, yours, ours, theirs — never your's).
⚠️
Battle 3 — my vs mine. Possessive adjective needs a noun after it: my book. Possessive pronoun stands alone: This book is mine. ❌ This is my's book. ✅ This book is mine.
⚠️
Battle 4 — reflexives. Use only when subject = object: I taught myself to cook. ❌ Please contact myself / Sarah and myself finished (sounds "fancy" but wrong) → ✅ Please contact me / Sarah and I finished.

Who vs Whom — the he/him Test

🧠
Swap the blank with he (→ who) or him (→ whom):
He called you. → Who called you? ✅
You called him. → Whom did you call? ✅
The engineer who was most familiar… (subject) vs the engineer whom I know well (object). In everyday speech people often use who for both — but exams and formal writing keep the distinction.

What Is a Preposition?

A preposition shows position, time, movement, or relationship between its noun and the rest of the sentence. It always comes before a noun (phrase) — that's literally what pre-position means!

📍 place: the cat on the table ⏱️ time: at 5 o'clock 🏃 movement: ran through the park 🤝 relation: proud of you
💡
UVic's memory trick: notice "position" is hiding inside the word pre-position — prepositions put nouns in their position in space/time.

⏳ AT / ON / IN for TIME — General → Specific

🗓️ IN — long periods→ 📅 ON — days & dates→ ⏰ AT — exact moments
INONAT
months: in July · years: in 2026 · seasons: in (the) summer · parts of day: in the morning/afternoon/evening · centuriesdays: on Monday · dates: on 30 July · events: on my birthday, on New Year's Dayclock times: at 5 o'clock · mealtimes: at lunchtime · at night · at Christmas (the whole holiday)
🧠
Funnel trick: IN (big bucket of time) → ON (one day slice) → AT (a single tick on the clock). ❌ in Monday / ❌ on 5 o'clock / ❌ at July.
Exception to memorize: it's at night (not "in night") — but in the morning.

📍 AT / ON / IN for PLACE — Point · Surface · Area

📍 AT — a point→ 🧱 ON — a surface→ 📦 IN — an enclosed area
Point vs Surface vs Area (Purdue OWL model)
AT — points & eventsON — surfaces & streetsIN — enclosed & large areas
at the door · at the bus stop · at the concert · at 25 Bank Road (address) · at school/work/home (activity)on the table · on the wall · on the 3rd floor · on Main Street · on the bus/train (public transport)in the box · in the room · in Karachi · in Pakistan · in the car/taxi (small vehicle)
✅
VOA rule: the trio works the same for time and place — general → specific: in (largest) → on (middle) → at (smallest). "I live in Karachi on Bank Road at number 25."

🔗 Fixed Combos that Power Your Phrases Note

Prepositions often come glued to an adjective or verb — memorize the pair, never translate literally:

Adjective + prep (powers AdjP!)Verb + prep
afraid of · good at · interested in · famous for · proud of · tired of · married todepend on · listen to · look at · wait for · arrive in a city / at a point
💡
This is exactly the AdjP from the Phrases tab: aware of the danger = adjective aware + prepositional phrase of the danger. Learn the combos → your adjective phrases come out right automatically.
